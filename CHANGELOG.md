Every release of Hodor, newest first. The rules for versions and releases are in [the conventions](docs/development/conventions.md#releases).

## [Unreleased]

### Added

- Hodor is licensed under the MIT License ([Decision 0038](docs/decisions/0038-the-license.md)); until now the repository had no license.

**Workloads prove who they are with their certificates**

- A tenant names the SPIFFE trust domains it trusts, each with the bundle of its authorities: a bundle an administrator uploads, a bundle fetched from the trust domain's SPIFFE bundle endpoint and kept current by the worker, or none for a trust domain whose certificates the mesh in front of Hodor checks. Two tenants may each trust a trust domain of the same name, such as `cluster.local`, and never see the other's. A bundle that does not read is refused, and a bundle endpoint that fails or answers an older bundle leaves the one in force; bundle endpoints are reached through the guarded outbound client, so one at a private address is refused. Every change is in the audit log. The administration interface manages them under `/trust-domains`, with `keys:read` and `keys:write`; the worker's new setting is `HODOR_TRUST_BUNDLE_POLL_INTERVAL_MS`. The certificates a proxy forwards are checked against them next in [Milestone 10](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-10-workloads-prove-who-they-are-with-their-certificates).
- A service account gets tokens with no secret of its own: its client is bound to the workload's SPIFFE identifier, `spiffe_id`, and proves who it is with the JWT-SVID SPIRE or the mesh gives it, under the new authentication method `spiffe_jwt` at the token, introspection and revocation endpoints. The JWT-SVID must be signed by a key of the bundle of the client's trust domain in the client's own tenant, be of exactly that identifier, and name the tenant's issuer alone as its audience; it is used once, so a workload sends a fresh one with each request. Hodor follows draft-ietf-oauth-spiffe-client-auth-03, an Internet-Draft that may still change; the discovery document advertises the method, and a change of the draft Hodor follows will be written here. Prefix matches of identifiers are not accepted, and the client needs `client_id` in the request.
- A workload behind a service mesh gets a token with no secret. The proxy in front of Hodor forwards the certificate the workload presented in mutual TLS, or the identity Linkerd asserts, in the one header the operator names in `HODOR_CLIENT_IDENTITY_HEADER`: `client-cert` (RFC 9440), `x-forwarded-client-cert` (Envoy, Istio, Consul) or `l5d-client-id` (Linkerd). None is read by default; the header is read only from the proxies of `HODOR_TRUSTED_PROXIES`, and only the element the nearest proxy added, so a header a caller writes is never believed. A client authenticates with `tls_client_auth` (RFC 8705), `spiffe_x509` (draft-ietf-oauth-spiffe-client-auth-03) or `linkerd_identity`, Hodor's own, bound as a service account to the `spiffe_id` or `linkerd_identity` an administrator gives it, each held by one service account in its tenant, and sends its `client_id` alone. A forwarded certificate must chain to the bundle of its identity's trust domain; the proxy's own check is relied on only for a trust domain marked as checked by the mesh. `HODOR_FORWARDED_HEADER` may now be `none`, for a sidecar trusted for the identity alone. The chart has `clientIdentityHeader`.
- A client may ask for access tokens bound to its certificate, as RFC 8705 writes it, with `tls_client_certificate_bound_access_tokens`. Its tokens then carry `cnf` with the SHA-256 of the certificate the proxy in front of Hodor forwarded, or Envoy's `Hash` of it when the proxy forwards only the names, as Istio does by default; a request of such a client with no certificate gets no token. A bound token is refused without its certificate, or with another, at UserInfo, the Authorization API, the SCIM server and token exchange, and the administration interface accepts no such token at all; introspection answers `cnf`, for a resource server to compare with the certificate its caller presented. A public client's refresh tokens are bound to its certificate too. The discovery document says `tls_client_certificate_bound_access_tokens` when a certificate header is named. A client using `linkerd_identity` cannot ask, since Linkerd forwards no certificate.
- Trust domains are managed from the admin console, under Security: a table of them with the state of each bundle and its last fetch, a page for one, and adding, replacing and removing them; and from the command-line tool, with `hodor-cli trust-domain add|list|show|replace|remove`. The console's client form and `hodor-cli client add` bind a service account's client to its SPIFFE identifier or its Linkerd identity, with the four methods of workloads, and ask for certificate-bound tokens (`--spiffe-id`, `--linkerd-identity`, `--bound-tokens`).
- The chart's `outboundAuthorities` names a ConfigMap of authorities that Hodor's outbound requests trust in place of the image's, for an identity provider or a bundle endpoint served under an authority of your own; `SSL_CERT_FILE` does the same outside the chart.
- The demonstration of Milestone 10, `deploy/demo/workload_identity.py`, runs on a kind cluster with Istio and SPIRE in continuous integration: a token with a JWT-SVID, then with no credential at all from a workload whose sidecar presents its certificate, bound to that certificate and refused without it, and what must be refused, refused.

**Hodor in a service mesh**

- The proxies of a service mesh can ask Hodor's decisions through `hodor-ext-authz`, a new binary in an image of its own, `ghcr.io/ortisun/hodor-ext-authz`, that serves Envoy's external authorization over gRPC for Istio, Consul, Envoy Gateway and Envoy. Each check becomes one call of the Authorization API's `evaluate`, asked as the client of the application the request is for, chosen by the destination's mesh identity; the resource and the action come from route rules in its configuration, or from the proxy's context for the route; the subject is the `sub` of the token the proxy checked, which must be of the tenant's issuer and name the audience of the application's resource server, so that a token for another application or an ID token is denied, or else the caller's mesh identity. It holds no secret: it proves who it is with its own mesh identity. It keeps no decision, and denies whenever it gets no answer. The chart installs it with `extAuthz.enabled`. How to set it up, and its measured latency, are in [its page](docs/operations/hodor-ext-authz.md).
- The Authorization API names a workload by its identity: `{"type": "workload_identity", "id": "spiffe://…"}`, or a Linkerd identity, is the service account whose clients are bound to that identity.
- Several clients of one service account may now be bound to the same SPIFFE identifier or Linkerd identity, one in each application, as `hodor-ext-authz` needs; a client of another service account is still refused.
- Linkerd can let in only the workloads whose service accounts hold a role on an application. `hodor-cli mesh linkerd-policy` writes, for one application, a `MeshTLSAuthentication` naming the Linkerd identities of the service accounts that hold a role on its resource server, directly, through a group or through a role that includes one, and an `AuthorizationPolicy` that requires it on the `Server` the operator names. It writes a file and applies nothing: the operator applies it, by hand or through the tool that deploys their cluster. Each run reads back the file of the last one and prints each identity added and removed, so a role taken away disappears from the next file and the change is said. A service account of another tenant never appears. The policy trails the roles until the file is applied again ([Decision 0059](docs/decisions/0059-linkerd-policies-from-roles.md)).

**Hodor in a service mesh**

- Hodor runs inside a service mesh with each person behind the ingress gateway still limited apart. `HODOR_GATEWAY_IDENTITIES` names the ingress gateways by their identity in the mesh, SPIFFE identifiers or Linkerd identities, and the forwarded address is then read only from a request whose caller, in the identity header the local proxy writes, is one of them; any other workload, and a request with no identity, is known by the proxy's address, whatever `X-Forwarded-For` it sends. The server refuses gateways it could never recognize. The chart's `mesh.provider`, `istio` or `linkerd`, injects the proxy, keeps the health checks on the operations port out of it, trusts the local proxy and its identity header, reads no forwarded address until `gatewayIdentities` names a gateway, opens what each mesh needs in the network policy, and lets the ports of `mesh.skipOutboundPorts` bypass the proxy. What each mesh hands Hodor was tried on kind, and Istio's ambient mode, where no identity reaches Hodor and a waypoint is never a trusted proxy, is described in the installation on Kubernetes.
- [The service mesh guide](docs/operations/service-mesh.md) shows, for Istio with sidecars and in ambient mode, Linkerd, Consul and Envoy Gateway, how the proxies check the tenant's tokens and ask Hodor's decisions through `hodor-ext-authz`, how Linkerd gets policies written from Hodor's roles, how workloads get their identity at Hodor and how Hodor runs inside, and marks each example with what ran it, from the demonstration in continuous integration to not tried. It says what is still missing: `mesh.provider` does not yet put `hodor-ext-authz` or `hodor-cert-manager-issuer` in the mesh, `hodor-ext-authz` does not check a token's audience, so the proxy must, and the latency target of `hodor-ext-authz` is still to be confirmed on a real server.

**An optional certificate authority per tenant**

- The tenants' signing keys can be kept in [OpenBao](docs/operations/keys-in-openbao.md). With `HODOR_KEY_PROVIDER=openbao` and OpenBao's address and sign-in, each new signing key is made in OpenBao's Transit secrets engine, never exportable, one key per tenant and version, and every token is signed there: Hodor keeps the key's name and its public key, never the key. Hodor signs in with Kubernetes authentication or an AppRole, and `deploy/openbao/hodor-policy.hcl` is the policy to give it, which allows making keys, reading their public keys and signing on Hodor's mount and nothing else. If OpenBao does not answer, nothing is signed with a key made there, and nothing is signed instead. Keys made before keep signing where they were made, and the keys of secrets at rest stay with `HODOR_SECRET_KEYS`. The chart has `keyProvider` values, the Docker installation passes the settings on, and the development stack runs OpenBao and makes its keys there when started with `HODOR_KEY_PROVIDER=openbao`.
- A tenant can have a [certificate authority](docs/architecture/contexts/keys.md#the-certificate-authority-of-a-tenant) of its own, on an installation that sets `HODOR_CERTIFICATE_AUTHORITY` to `on` (off by default). Its key is made in OpenBao and never leaves it; the local provider is accepted only under `development`. It is a root signed by its own key, or an intermediate whose request the tenant's own root signs, and its certificate carries name constraints that permit only the tenant's trust domains named for it. Its bundle is published at `/t/{tenant}/certificate-authority/bundle` as a SPIFFE bundle endpoint, and Hodor's trust domains of those names trust it at once. Two permissions, `certificate-authority:read` and `certificate-authority:write`, and an audit event for every signature ([Decision 0062](docs/decisions/0062-the-certificate-authority-of-a-tenant.md)).
- The tenant's certificate authority [signs the intermediates of its meshes](docs/architecture/contexts/keys.md#signing-intermediates) from their certificate signing requests, through the administration interface or `hodor-cli authority sign`: only an authority with no deeper path, only names within the tenant's trust domains, name constraints that only narrow, keys of a known kind and size and a lifetime within the tenant's bound, each refusal saying why. Hodor writes the path length, the key usages and the name constraints itself. Withdrawing an intermediate stops Hodor trusting its key from the next request, and the key is never signed again. A new permission, `certificate-authority:sign`, and a built-in role holding only it, `certificate_signer`, let a workload sign with a key of that one scope and nothing else ([Decision 0071](docs/decisions/0071-signing-intermediates.md)).
- cert-manager keeps the intermediates of a tenant's meshes signed by its certificate authority through [`hodor-cert-manager-issuer`](docs/operations/hodor-cert-manager-issuer.md), a new binary in an image of its own, `ghcr.io/ortisun/hodor-cert-manager-issuer`. It defines `HodorIssuer` and `HodorClusterIssuer`, and signs through Hodor only the `CertificateRequest`s addressed to them that are approved and ask for an authority; a denied request, one that is not for an authority and one whose issuer is missing are marked and never sent, a refusal of Hodor's is named in the request's condition with its code, and while Hodor cannot be reached the request waits and is tried again. Its client proves who it is with its mesh identity or a key of its own and gets an access token for the new audience `urn:hodor:certificate-authority` with the scope `certificate-authority:sign`, which the administration interface's signing operation now takes, and no other operation does. The chart installs it with `certManagerIssuer.enabled`, beside Hodor or alone in a cluster of meshes, with its own permissions in the cluster.
- A tenant's certificate authority may leave URIs unconstrained, with `uri_constraints` `false` when it is created, so that a Linkerd mesh of the tenant accepts the SPIFFE identities of its other meshes: Linkerd's proxy refuses every URI under an authority that constrains URIs. It may also permit DNS names besides its trust domains, `additional_dns_names`, such as `istio-system.svc`, without which istiod's own certificate, signed by the mesh's intermediate, is refused by the Kubernetes interface and by the sidecars ([Decision 0076](docs/decisions/0076-names-an-authority-leaves-to-its-meshes.md)). Authorities made before keep their constraints. The chart turns the authority on with `certificateAuthority.enabled`, and `certificateAuthority.development` for a machine of one's own.
- The demonstration of Milestone 12, `deploy/demo/certificate_authority.py`, runs in continuous integration on two kind clusters, one with Istio and one with Linkerd, under one tenant's root: the authority off, refused on the local key provider, created on OpenBao; cert-manager keeping Istio's `cacerts` and Linkerd's identity issuer through `hodor-cert-manager-issuer` and renewing one with no one acting; a workload of the Istio cluster calling one of the Linkerd cluster over mutual TLS; requests for another tenant's trust domain, for no authority and past the bound refused; an intermediate withdrawn and its workloads refused at once; every signature in the audit log.

**Requests that say what they need**

- A client may send its authorization request as a request object, a JSON Web Token given as `request` (OpenID Connect Core, section 6, and RFC 9101). It is checked with the client's own keys, signed with `ES256`, `RS256` or `PS256`; a client's `request_object_signing_alg` may name one of them, or `none` for a client that sends unsigned objects, which no other client may. `iss`, `client_id`, `aud`, `exp` and `nbf` are checked when given, and a signed object must name its client as `iss`. The parameters inside the object are the request: those outside are ignored but for `client_id`, and `response_type`, `client_id` and `scope` repeated outside must say the same. Every check of the authorization endpoint then applies to what the object holds. An object signed by another client's key, by a client of another tenant, or not signed for a client that names an algorithm, is refused with `invalid_request_object`. Discovery names `request_parameter_supported` and `request_object_signing_alg_values_supported`; the administration interface and `hodor-cli client add --request-object-signing-alg` set the client's algorithm. The OpenID conformance suite's request object tests of the Basic plan now pass instead of being skipped. References to a request object, `request_uri`, come next in [Milestone 13](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-13-requests-that-say-what-they-need).
- A client may push its authorization request to Hodor first, at `/t/{slug}/oauth2/par` (RFC 9126), proving who it is as at the token endpoint, and send the browser with only the reference it gets back as `request_uri`. The client proves who it is by any method of the token endpoint, a certificate or an identity forwarded by a trusted proxy included. The request is checked when it is pushed, every check of the authorization endpoint, its request object and its `id_token_hint` included, and a failure is answered to the client at once, never at a redirect address. A pushed request keeps its response mode, `form_post` included, and the person its hint names. It is kept as a pending authorization request, found by the hash of its reference, which is stored nowhere; it lives 60 seconds unless the tenant sets 10 to 600, a client has at most five waiting, and a reference is used once, even when it is brought twice at once. A reference used twice, past its life, of another client or of another tenant is refused with `invalid_request_uri`. A client, or a tenant for all of its clients, may require every request to be pushed, and a request that was not is refused with `invalid_request`. Discovery names `pushed_authorization_request_endpoint` and the tenant's `require_pushed_authorization_requests`; the administration interface sets the client's requirement and, at `/pushed-authorization-requests`, the tenant's life and requirement, as do `hodor-cli client add --require-pushed-authorization-requests` and `hodor-cli tenant pushed-requests`. References at an address of the client's come next.
- A client may keep its request object at an address of its own and send only that address as `request_uri` (OpenID Connect Core, section 6.2). An administrator registers where the client keeps them, as `request_uris` through the administration interface or `hodor-cli client add --request-uri`: at most ten HTTPS addresses with a path, each refused when it leads to a private network. Hodor fetches an address only when it starts with one the client registered, through the guarded outbound client, at most 16 kibibytes within 5 seconds, following no redirect; a hash after `#` must be the SHA-256 of what was fetched. What comes back is checked as any request object. An address the client did not register is never fetched, and one that leads to a private network, does not answer in time, answers too much or answers content of another hash is refused with `invalid_request_uri`. Nothing fetched is kept. Discovery now says `request_uri_parameter_supported` and `require_request_uri_registration`.
- The authorization endpoint honors the parameters of a request it ignored until now. `id_token_hint` names the person the application believes is signed in: it must be an ID token the tenant issued to that client, checked by the tenant's keys and issuer but not its expiry, or the answer is `invalid_request`, so a hint signed by another tenant is refused; a browser signed in as someone else is answered `login_required` with `prompt=none`, and is asked to sign in otherwise. `prompt=create` sends the person to the registration page, which then signs them in and goes back to the application, in a tenant open to registration; a tenant closed to it does not support the value, answers `invalid_request` and leaves `create` out of its discovery document. `display` takes `page`, `popup` or `touch`, which the pages already fit, and discovery names them. A client has three new settings of OpenID Connect Registration, through the administration interface: `default_max_age` and `default_acr_values`, which a request without `max_age` or `acr_values` takes, and `require_auth_time`, which every ID token already satisfies. UserInfo also reads the access token from a form body (RFC 6750, section 2.2), so the conformance test `oidcc-userinfo-post-body` passes without a warning.

**Answers through the browser**

- A client may ask for its answer as a form. With `response_mode=form_post`, the authorization endpoint answers with a page of Hodor's own that posts the code, the state and the issuer to the client's redirect address, and nothing else, as OAuth 2.0 Form Post Response Mode says; an error goes back the same way, and a request kept while the person signs in or is asked for consent keeps its mode. The page runs only its own script, named by its hash in its content security policy, loads nothing, cannot be framed, is never cached and sends no `Referer`; a browser that runs no script shows a button that sends the same form. Discovery names `query` and `form_post` in `response_modes_supported`. Any other response mode, those not built yet included, is still refused with `invalid_request`.
- A client's ID tokens may be signed with `ES256`, `RS256`, `PS256` or `EdDSA`, its `id_token_signed_response_alg`, and its UserInfo answers too: a client with `userinfo_signed_response_alg` gets UserInfo as a JSON Web Token (`application/jwt`) with `iss` and `aud`, signed with the tenant's key of that algorithm (OpenID Connect Core, section 5.3.2); without it, UserInfo answers JSON as before. Discovery names the four in `id_token_signing_alg_values_supported` and the new `userinfo_signing_alg_values_supported`. Another name, `none` among them, is refused when the client is saved. Set through the administration interface and `hodor-cli client add --id-token-algorithm` and `--userinfo-signed-response-alg`.
- EdDSA keys on Ed25519 (RFC 8037), made by the local key provider and by OpenBao, and published in the tenant's key set as `OKP` keys. The published key set now names a key of each of the four algorithms, PS256 with an RSA key of its own. **An installation that keeps its signing keys in OpenBao must write Hodor's policy again** from `deploy/openbao/hodor-policy.hcl`, which now allows keys of type `ed25519`; until then OpenBao refuses the first EdDSA key a tenant needs.

**Requests that say what they need**

- A person keeps the standard claims of OpenID Connect: given, middle and family names, nickname, the addresses of their page, picture and website, gender, birth date, time zone, telephone number with whether it is verified, and postal address. Each is optional and has its bound. A person edits them on the new profile page of the account, an administrator through `PUT /users/{user_id}/claims` of the administration interface, an operator with `hodor-cli user claims`; an identity provider gives them when it creates the person, OpenID Connect providers by their own names, GitHub its picture and site, directories and SAML providers through claim attributes an administrator names; SCIM's names, nickname, profile address, time zone, telephone number and postal address are now the person's. UserInfo answers them, the scopes `address` and `phone` are offered, and claim mappers can read them. A telephone number is never a login identifier, and a person cannot vouch for their own. No event or log carries a value ([Decision 0065](docs/decisions/0065-where-the-standard-claims-come-from.md)).
- A client asks for the person's claims one by one with the `claims` parameter of OpenID Connect: in the ID token, at UserInfo or both, under their own names. A claim is given only when a scope consented to for the client covers it: for an application of the tenant's own, a scope the client may ask for; for another, one the person agreed to. It is never given otherwise, and the person is not asked for it. A claim that would make the ID token pass its bound of 4096 bytes, shared with the claim mappers, is left out, never cut. An `acr` asked as essential steps the person up as `acr_values` does, and a `sub` asked with a value is honored: the session of someone else is never used. `claims_locales` is accepted, and discovery says `claims_parameter_supported` ([Decision 0068](docs/decisions/0068-claims-of-every-consented-scope.md)).
- A user created through the administration interface may be given a username.

### Changed

- The given and family names SCIM set move from the provisioning context to the person, by migration 0098; a name longer than 200 characters is left for SCIM to send again. Claim mappers may no longer name a standard claim of OpenID Connect, which UserInfo now writes: one saved earlier under such a name writes nothing until it is renamed.

**Clients that register themselves**

- A tenant can let clients register themselves, and decides who may and what they may become. Registration is off in every tenant until an administrator turns it on, naming the application registered clients belong to, which may not be first party, the ways a registration may be allowed (an initial access token, a software statement, or openly), how many clients that application may hold, up to 1000, and one registration policy for every way: the grants, response types, scopes, authentication methods and subject types a registration may name, and the hosts its addresses may name. `client_credentials`, token exchange and the methods of workloads are never registered. While registration is off, discovery names no registration endpoint and nothing of it is reachable. Initial access tokens, `hodor_iat_…`, are shown once and kept as hashes, expire after 1 hour to 90 days, allow a number of registrations, and are refused in one way once used up, expired, revoked, or presented to another tenant. The administration interface manages all of it under `/client-registration`, with `applications:read` and `applications:write`, and so does `hodor-cli client-registration show|on|off|token`. The registration endpoint that reads these settings comes next in [Milestone 15](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-15-clients-that-register-themselves).

### Fixed

- A refusal of a certificate signing request, or of a certificate installed on an intermediate authority, through the administration interface, named only its code and a general message, not its reason, which the documentation of the interface promised: the name outside the trust domains, the size of the key, the bound on the lifetime. The problem's `detail` now names the reason for these refusals, which are about what the caller sent, and `hodor-cert-manager-issuer` writes it into the request's condition.
- A decision of the Authorization API took twice the time it needs: every transaction checked its connection and started in two more round trips to the database, and a decision opened eleven of them. A transaction now starts in one round trip, a connection used less than a second before is not checked again, and a decision reads the token's client, its application and its resource server together, and a person with their groups and organizations together, in seven transactions. Nothing more is kept between decisions than before. A request may now fail on a connection used less than a second before the database restarted ([the cost of a decision](docs/architecture/contexts/authorization.md#the-cost-of-a-decision)).
- A webhook endpoint could receive an event that happened before it was registered or given the event's type, when the event was dispatched just after. Each subscription now keeps when it began, and an endpoint receives only the events that happened since.

## [0.3.0] - 2026-11-25

A pre-release that gathers people in groups and organizations, gives services and agents identities of their own, and builds what 0.2.0 left out. It holds the four parts [Decision 0027](docs/decisions/0027-what-0.3.0-holds.md) set for it: what a deployment behind a load balancer needs ([Milestone 6](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-6-hodor-runs-behind-a-load-balancer-and-cleans-up-after-itself)), groups, organizations and SCIM ([Milestone 7](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-7-groups-organizations-and-scim)), workloads, agents and webhooks ([Milestone 8](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-8-workloads-agents-and-webhooks)), and the parts of OpenID Connect, email sign-in, access lists and enrollment that 0.2.0 left out ([Milestone 9](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-9-what-020-left-out)).

**0.3.0 is not ready to hold real accounts.** What stands in the way, besides what 0.1.0 and 0.2.0 listed and still holds:

- still no limit on the number of requests to the token, revocation, introspection and authorization endpoints, nor to the Authorization API, and still no cross-origin headers;
- the OpenID conformance plans, back-channel logout among them, pass in continuous integration, but Hodor is not certified; the suite's own pairwise tests need dynamic client registration, which Hodor does not have, so pairwise clients are tried through the basic and back-channel plans instead;
- the published image and chart are built for the first time by this release's tag; they are tried by hand after it;
- no directory of the world (Entra ID, Okta, a human resources system) has talked to the SCIM server, and no webhook endpoint or application of the world has received Hodor's webhooks or logout tokens; the demonstrations stand in for them;
- a session that runs out by itself, idle or at its absolute limit, sends no logout token;
- the console does not manage access control lists, claim mappers, email sign-in, pairwise clients, or the binding of a client to a workload; the administration interface and the command-line tool do.

The [progress](docs/product/progress.md) page lists every known gap.

### Added

**Behind a load balancer, and what is shipped**

- Hodor can sit behind load balancers and web servers and still count each client apart. Name them in `HODOR_TRUSTED_PROXIES` and Hodor reads the client address they forward in `X-Forwarded-For`, or in `Forwarded` with `HODOR_FORWARDED_HEADER`. The limits on sign-in attempts, sessions and the audit log then use that address. A forwarded header from anyone else is ignored. The Docker installation trusts its own web server, and the chart has `trustedProxies` and `forwardedHeader`.
- The worker removes what stopped being useful: ended sessions, used and expired links, delivered messages, expired codes, revoked grants and refresh tokens, withdrawn consents, and more. Each kind is kept for a while after it stops being useful, 7 days for sessions and 30 for messages, for example, and `HODOR_CLEANUP_RETENTION_DAYS` changes how long. The audit log is never touched.
- Each release publishes its image, `ghcr.io/ortisun/hodor:<version>`, for `linux/amd64` and `linux/arm64`, and its Helm chart, `oci://ghcr.io/ortisun/charts/hodor`. Both are signed with `cosign`, and the image carries its software bill of materials. The installation pages say how to check the signatures. The chart now uses the published image unless `image.repository` says otherwise.

**Groups, organizations and SCIM**

- Groups of users in a tenant, with groups inside groups, no cycles and a depth the tenant chooses with `hodor-cli tenant group-depth`.
- Organizations inside a tenant, with a tree of units and departments up to ten levels deep, and people as members of several organizations, each in some of its units.
- The administration interface manages groups, organizations, their units and members, and invitations, under four new permissions: `groups:read`, `groups:write`, `organizations:read` and `organizations:write`. `hodor-cli group` and `hodor-cli organization` do the same from the command line, and the admin console from its new screens of groups and organizations. A user's screen in the console shows their groups and organizations.
- Roles given to a group, held by every member of the group and of the groups inside it, and roles given in an organization, which count only for its members and only when the question names it. `GET /groups/{group_id}/role-assignments` lists a group's roles, and `hodor-cli role assign` takes `--group` and `--organization`.
- A client names the organization a person signs in for with `organization`. Only a member gets a code, and every token of the grant carries the organization as `org_id`, until the person leaves it. The scope `groups` puts the identifiers of the person's groups, the nested ones included, in the access token and at UserInfo.
- Invitations to an organization by email. The person accepts from the link: with a new account and a password if the address has none, or signed in as the account that has it.
- A SCIM 2.0 server at `/t/{slug}/scim/v2`, so that a directory such as Entra ID or Okta, or a human resources system, creates, reads, replaces and lists a tenant's people and groups, with the enterprise extension, filters, sorting and pages. A tenant opens it by registering a resource server with the audience `urn:hodor:scim` and the scope `scim`, and gives the directory a client allowed that scope. Addresses set through SCIM count as verified. What SCIM changes is in the audit log with the directory's client as the actor. Changing through `PATCH`, bulk requests and deleting people come later.
- SCIM clients change people and groups with `PATCH`, send up to 100 changes at once to `/Bulk`, and guard their changes with versions: `If-Match` refuses a change for a version that is no longer the resource's.
- When a directory deprovisions a person, with `DELETE` or `active: false`, the tenant's policy decides whether they are deactivated or deleted, and their sessions and tokens stop working at once. The policy is set with `hodor-cli tenant scim-deprovisioning` or `PUT /scim-deprovisioning` of the administration interface, and is `deactivate` unless changed.
- **The demonstration of Milestone 7**, `deploy/demo/organizations.py`, run in continuous integration: a group inside another and a cycle refused, an invitation to an organization accepted from its link, a role held through a group until the member is taken out, a sign-in whose tokens carry `org_id`, and a person provisioned through SCIM and deactivated, whose session and refresh token end at once.

**Workloads, agents and webhooks**

- Service accounts and agents: principals of a tenant that are not people, each with a name and the people who own it. They never sign in. A client of the client credentials grant can act as one: its tokens then name it in `sub` and say `principal_kind`, and the roles assigned to it count in the Authorization API. Disabling or deleting one stops its tokens at once, at introspection, UserInfo and the Authorization API, and its clients get no new ones. The administration interface manages them under `/service-accounts` and `/agents` with two new permissions, `workloads:read` and `workloads:write`, and so does `hodor-cli service-account` and `hodor-cli agent`; `principal` binds a client to one. Clients without one work as before.
- Webhooks. A tenant registers up to 20 endpoints and subscribes each to the events it wants, such as `identity.user_created` or `session.started`. The worker sends each event to its endpoints, signed as [Standard Webhooks](https://www.standardwebhooks.com/) say, so any of their libraries checks it. It keeps trying with longer and longer waits, sets a delivery aside after 8 attempts, and delivers it again when asked. The secret is shown once and can be rotated with an overlap during which both secrets sign. An address that leads to a private network is refused when it is registered and again whenever it is called. The administration interface has nine new operations under `webhooks:read` and `webhooks:write`; the worker has new settings, `HODOR_WEBHOOK_*`, and needs `HODOR_SECRET_KEYS` to send.
- API keys for service accounts and agents: `hodor_ak_` followed by 64 hexadecimal characters, so that a secret scanner recognizes one, kept as a hash and shown once. A key has scopes, an expiry, a time of last use, and rotates with an overlap or is revoked. The administration interface and the Authorization API take it in place of a token; at the administration interface it does no more than its scopes and its holder's roles allow, and `hodor-cli admin grant` gives a workload a built-in role. Keys are managed under `/service-accounts/{id}/api-keys` and `/agents/{id}/api-keys`, and with `hodor-cli service-account key` and `hodor-cli agent key`.
- Token exchange (RFC 8693): an agent's client exchanges a person's token for one the agent holds on their behalf. The new token names the person in `sub` and the agent in `act`, for one audience the client's exchange rules allow, with scopes that can only narrow, and it stops as soon as the person's token, their grant or the agent does. A chain holds at most three actors, and every exchange is in the audit log with its chain. The administration interface and `hodor-cli client add --exchange-rule` give an agent's client the grant and its rules, and discovery names the grant.
- The admin console manages service accounts and agents, with their owners, API keys and roles, and webhooks, with their event types, secrets and deliveries, from new screens under Directory and Audit. An API key and an endpoint's secret are shown once, in the dialog that follows the answer that made them. A client's page names the service account or agent it acts as.
- `hodor-cli webhook` registers, lists, subscribes and removes a tenant's webhook endpoints, rotates their secrets, lists deliveries and delivers a set-aside one again.
- **The demonstration of Milestone 8**, `deploy/demo/workloads.py`, run in continuous integration: a service account evaluated with its token and with its API key, an agent exchanging a person's token for one that names the agent in `act`, a wider exchange refused, a webhook that arrives signed, is set aside while its receiver is stopped and is delivered again, and a key and a webhook of one tenant refused by another. The development stack has a receiver of webhooks, `webhook-receiver`, for it.

**What 0.2.0 left out**

- Access control lists in the native engine: an entry lets one user, client, group, service account or agent do what one permission says to one resource, such as reading the document `d-42`. Entries allow only on their own resource, are read beside the roles at every decision that names a resource, and stop allowing a person on the next decision once they leave a group an entry names. The administration interface grants, lists, reads and removes them under two new permissions, `access-entries:read` and `access-entries:write`, and so does `hodor-cli authorization access-entry`.
- Signing in with a code or a link sent by email, each turned on per tenant with `hodor-cli tenant email-sign-in` or `PUT /email-sign-in` of the administration interface, and off unless turned on. The sign-in page offers "email me a code" and "email me a link". A code or a link works once, for ten minutes, and only in the browser that asked for it: a link opened anywhere else signs nobody in. Asking answers the same whoever the address belongs to, and wrong codes count towards the same lockout as wrong passwords. A second factor is still asked for after it.
- A person who has no second factor, in a tenant that requires one, sets one up while signing in. After the password, the sign-in page offers an authenticator application from its QR code, a security key or a passkey, shows the recovery codes once, and the sign-in completes at multi-factor assurance. Until now such a person could not sign in at all. Nothing gets past the step but setting up a factor, and it is never offered to someone who has one, nor when an application asks a signed-in person to prove more.
- Back-channel logout. A client registers a `backchannel_logout_uri`, through the administration interface or `hodor-cli client add --backchannel-logout-uri`, and when a person's session ends, by signing out, logout initiated by the application, an administrator, a password reset or deprovisioning, the worker sends every client the person signed in to with that session a logout token at that address, signed with the tenant's key, carrying `sub` and the session's `sid`, as [OpenID Connect Back-Channel Logout 1.0](https://openid.net/specs/openid-connect-backchannel-1_0.html) says. It keeps trying with longer and longer waits and sets a delivery aside after 8 attempts. An address that leads to a private network is refused when it is set and again whenever it is called. Discovery says `backchannel_logout_supported` and `backchannel_logout_session_supported`, and the back-channel logout plan of the OpenID conformance suite runs in continuous integration. **The worker now needs `HODOR_PUBLIC_URL`**, the server's, to send logout tokens; the installation packages give it.
- Pairwise subject identifiers. A client that signs people in can be made `pairwise`, through the administration interface or `hodor-cli client add --subject-type pairwise`: it then knows each person by an identifier of its sector alone, the host of its redirect addresses or of a `sector_identifier_uri` that Hodor fetches once, through the guarded outbound client, and that must list every redirect address. Applications of one sector see the same identifier, and applications of different sectors cannot tell that they share a person. The identifier is an HMAC under a secret of the tenant, kept sealed and never changed; the ID token, the access token, UserInfo, introspection, token exchange and logout tokens all give it, `sid` included, while roles, the Authorization API and the audit log keep the user identifier. Discovery offers `pairwise`.
- Claim mappers per client: a client's ID tokens, access tokens and answers of UserInfo can carry claims taken from the person's name, username, email address or locale, their groups, whether they are in one group, the organization they signed in for, or a constant. A mapper may not name a claim Hodor sets, such as `sub`, `iss` or `acr`, and the mappers of a client are refused when they are saved if together they could make a token too large, so no token is ever refused for it. They are managed under `/clients/{client_id}/claim-mappers` of the administration interface and with `hodor-cli client mapper`.
- **The demonstration of Milestone 9**, `deploy/demo/what_was_left_out.py`, run in continuous integration: two applications of two sectors that know one person by two `sub`, a claim mapper that puts a group into the ID token, a logout token for each application when the person signs out, a code and a link by email each refused the second time, an authenticator application set up from its QR code during a sign-in under the second factor policy, and a document shared with a group through an access control list. Browser tests now sign in with a code and a link by email and set up a second factor during a sign-in, and the cross-tenant suite tries a pairwise ID token at the other tenant's end-session endpoint and a lockout by wrong codes.

### Fixed

- A link to the console's screens of applications, clients and authorization, or a reload of one, answered with not found; the server now answers every screen the console has with its page.
- Two processes of the same kind, such as two workers or two replicas of the server, sent their metrics as one series, so that each overwrote the other's counts in VictoriaMetrics. Each process now names itself with `service.instance.id`, a random identifier made when it starts.

## [0.2.0] - 2026-11-23

A pre-release that lets applications ask Hodor who someone is, and whether they may do something. It holds the three parts [Decision 0023](docs/decisions/0023-what-0.2.0-holds.md) set for it: the OAuth 2.x authorization server and OpenID Provider ([Milestone 3](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-3-applications-ask-hodor-who-someone-is)), multi-factor authentication with passkeys and step-up ([Milestone 4](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-4-strong-authentication)), and the authorization engine with Open Policy Agent ([Milestone 5](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-5-the-authorization-engine)). Each part is cut to what makes it whole; what each leaves to its phase is in that decision.

**0.2.0 is not ready to hold real accounts.** What stands in the way, besides what 0.1.0 listed and still holds:

- no limit on the number of requests to the token, revocation, introspection and authorization endpoints, nor to the Authorization API;
- no cross-origin headers, so an application running in a browser cannot call the token endpoint or UserInfo;
- the OpenID conformance plans pass in continuous integration, but Hodor is not certified: four tests wait for a person to review their screenshots, and nobody of the OpenID Foundation has seen the results;
- request objects, the `claims` parameter, pairwise subjects and back-channel logout are not supported;
- no physical security key and no authenticator built into a device were tried; WebAuthn and passkeys were tested with software and virtual authenticators;
- the journeys of the authorization endpoint, consent, logout and step-up, and the console's new screens, were not tried by hand in a browser;
- no application and no Open Policy Agent instance of the world has talked to Hodor yet; the demonstrations and the conformance suite stand in for them.

The [progress](docs/product/progress.md) page lists every known gap, with "Before Milestone 3 is used anywhere real".

### Added

**Applications ask Hodor who someone is**

- **Signing keys per tenant**, kept sealed, rotated by the worker on a schedule and published as a key set at `/t/{slug}/oauth2/jwks`, with the next key published a day before it signs and the old one kept until its tokens expire. `hodor-cli keys list|rotate|revoke` and the administration interface.
- **Applications, clients and resource servers**: confidential and public clients, redirect addresses compared exactly, client secrets shown once and kept as hashes, keys for `private_key_jwt`, resource servers with their scopes. Managed through the administration interface, `hodor-cli application|client|resource-server` and the console.
- **The authorization endpoint** with PKCE, one-use authorization codes and a sign-in in the middle when there is no session; **the token endpoint** with the four client authentication methods and access tokens as JWT (RFC 9068); **refresh tokens** that rotate on every use, whose reuse ends their family; **client credentials**.
- **The OpenID Provider**: ID tokens signed with ES256 or, per client, RS256; UserInfo; discovery and the authorization server metadata; `nonce`, `prompt`, `max_age`, `login_hint`, `ui_locales`; `acr` and `amr` (RFC 8176).
- **Consent** for third-party applications, on a page of the hosted pages, kept with its history and withdrawn by the person or an administrator, which ends the grants it allowed.
- **Logout initiated by the relying party**, with registered addresses to return to and a confirmation when the request does not name the browser's session.
- **Revocation and introspection** (RFC 7009 and RFC 7662), with a register of the access tokens issued, so that a revoked grant or family makes its access tokens inactive.
- **The OpenID conformance suite in continuous integration**: the Basic OpenID Provider and Config OpenID Provider plans of the OpenID Foundation's suite run against the image on every pull request ([conformance](docs/operations/conformance.md)).
- **The demonstration of Milestone 3**, `deploy/demo/applications.py`, run in continuous integration: a third-party application registered with the command-line tool, the consent page, the ID token and UserInfo, a refresh token whose reuse ends its family, a token a client gets for itself, revocation and introspection, a rotated key, and a client, a code and a token of one tenant refused by another.

**Strong authentication**

- **Authenticator applications (TOTP)** as a second factor, with the secret sealed and every code good once; **recovery codes**, ten per person, each good once; **WebAuthn security keys**, checked by Hodor's own code ([Decision 0024](docs/decisions/0024-webauthn-checks.md)); **passkeys** that sign a person in alone, without an identifier or a password.
- **A tenant policy that requires a second factor**, `hodor-cli tenant second-factor`.
- **Assurance levels and step-up**: a tenant names the `acr` levels applications ask for, single-factor, multi-factor and phishing-resistant; an application asks with `acr_values` or `max_age`, and a person signed in is asked only for what their session lacks. Tokens say the level reached. Set the levels with `hodor-cli tenant assurance-levels` or the administration interface.
- **A person manages their own factors** on `/account/security`, after proving themselves again; an administrator removes a person's factors through the administration interface and the console.
- **The demonstration of Milestone 4**, `deploy/demo/strong_authentication.py`, run in continuous integration: an authenticator application, recovery codes, a tenant that requires a second factor, a passkey alone, a step-up asked for by an application, and an administrator removing a person's factors.

**The authorization engine**

- **The engine contract**: one engine answers for each resource server of a tenant, a failing engine never answers allow, and the tenant always comes from the caller's credential.
- **Native role-based access control**: permissions per resource server, roles with hierarchy and composites, assignments to users and clients for the tenant or an application. The fixed roles of the administration interface became roles of this engine, unchanged.
- **Conditions in Common Expression Language** on the permissions of a role, checked and bounded when the role is saved ([Decision 0025](docs/decisions/0025-condition-language.md)).
- **The Authorization API**: `evaluate`, `batch` and `simulate` under `/t/{slug}/api/v1/authorization/`, called with access tokens, the explanation only for callers allowed to see it.
- **Open Policy Agent as an engine**, over its Data API, with a circuit breaker and every failure an indeterminate answer; **signed bundles** pulled by each instance with a credential of its own; **the policy lifecycle**: versions validated and tested with the `opa` binary in a sandbox of the worker ([Decision 0026](docs/decisions/0026-policy-checks-in-a-sandbox.md)), simulated, published and rolled back with `hodor-cli policy draft|validate|test|simulate|publish|rollback|status`; a decision from an instance that runs an older revision five minutes after a new one is published is `indeterminate`.
- **The record of decisions**, for both engines, per tenant full, sampled or deny-only, masked by path and kept for a number of days, partitioned by day.
- **Managing authorization through the administration interface and the command line**: the permissions of a resource server, roles with their inclusions and conditions, who holds them, which engine answers for each resource server, and policy versions drafted, checked, tested and simulated, under the new permissions `roles:read|write`, `bindings:read|write` and `policies:read|write`. `hodor-cli authorization permission|role|assign|revoke|bind|unbind|bindings`. Publishing and rolling back a policy stay commands of the operator, who alone holds the key that signs bundles.
- **An Authorization screen in the admin console**: the tenant's roles with their conditions, changed as text and checked by the server; the engine of each resource server and removing a binding; what is published for Open Policy Agent and whether every instance runs it; the latest decisions. A person's and a client's roles are on their pages, and can be taken away.
- **The demonstration of Milestone 5**, `deploy/demo/authorization.py`, run in continuous integration after the other four: roles given to a person and a client, decisions asked for with a token, a condition that changes the answer, a resource server answered by Open Policy Agent with policies published, simulated and rolled back, a stopped instance whose answer is never allow, and the record of decisions.

### Changed

- **Secrets are kept under a data key of their tenant**, a key version of the keys context, and the keys of `HODOR_SECRET_KEYS` only wrap keys. After upgrading from 0.1.0, run `hodor-cli secrets reseal` once: it moves the secrets 0.1.0 kept and then refuses their old format. Until it runs, they open as before.
- **`hodor-cli policy-agent publish` is gone.** Bundles are published only from tested policy versions, with `hodor-cli policy publish`.
- **The image holds the `opa` binary**, 46 MB, pinned by checksum; the chart gives the worker 512 MiB of memory instead of 256 MiB.

### Fixed

- **Building the image where other branches were built.** The shared cargo cache of the image's build compared modification times and could build code of another checkout; the build now marks the workspace's files as changed first.

## [0.1.0] - 2026-11-22

The first release, a pre-release: the walking skeleton of [Milestone 1](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-1-walking-skeleton) and signing in with external identities, [Milestone 2](docs/product/epics.md#milestone-2-signing-in-with-external-identities). Hodor lets people into a tenant; it does not yet let applications ask Hodor who someone is.

0.2.0 comes when three more parts are built: the OAuth 2.x authorization server and OpenID Provider, multi-factor authentication with passkeys and step-up, and the authorization engine with Open Policy Agent. These same contents were first published as `v1.0.0` by mistake, before those parts; that tag and release were withdrawn the same day, before any image or chart was published.

**0.1.0 is not ready to hold real accounts.** What stands in the way:

- the development stack's passwords and keys are written in the repository, and it speaks plain HTTP: see [before anything real](docs/operations/quick-start.md#before-anything-real);
- behind a load balancer every person shares one limit of attempts, since a forwarded address is not trusted yet;
- SAML has not been reviewed by anyone outside the project, which [Decision 0019](docs/decisions/0019-saml-signature-verification.md) makes a condition for production;
- no connector has been tried against the real provider, and LDAP was tried against OpenLDAP only;
- sessions, verification tokens, sign-ins, attempt counters and message records that expired are never removed;
- no image and no chart are published: both are built from a checkout of the tag.

The [progress](docs/product/progress.md) page lists every known gap.

### Added

- **Tenants**, each isolated in the database by row-level security, created and managed with `hodor-cli`, resolved from the address of every request.
- **Users and passwords**: registration and password recovery through a link sent by email, Argon2id hashing under a limit of memory and concurrency, sign-in in steps driven by a flow engine, lockout per identifier and per address.
- **Sessions** tied to the browser that started them, with signing out and ending one's other sessions.
- **The hosted pages**, one React application served by `hodor-server` under a strict Content Security Policy, in English and Brazilian Portuguese, tested in a browser for accessibility.
- **The administration interface**, under fixed roles, with its OpenAPI description; the first administrator is made with `hodor-cli admin bootstrap`. **The admin console** on the administrator's Hodor session.
- **The audit log**: every event of a tenant recorded in a chain of hashes, written by `hodor-worker` from a transactional outbox, and checked with `hodor-cli audit verify`.
- **Email** through a queue that the worker delivers over SMTP, with retries.
- **Signing in with external identities**: any OpenID Connect provider, with recipes for Google, Microsoft Entra ID and Keycloak; GitHub; LDAP directories; and SAML identity providers, with Hodor as the service provider. An external identity is never let into an existing account because of its email address.
- **SAML checked behind a narrow profile of Hodor's own** with `xml-sec`: a corpus of more than sixty attacks, each refused for its own reason; responses checked as they arrive, with only the answer kept; a receipt that ties the finish of a sign-in to the browser that carried the response.
- **Secrets kept encrypted at rest** with AES-256-GCM, bound to their tenant and purpose, with keys that can be changed.
- **One guarded client for outbound requests**: public addresses only, no redirects, limits on size and time.
- **Telemetry** over OpenTelemetry: traces to VictoriaTraces, metrics to VictoriaMetrics.
- **A Compose stack for development** with a development OpenID Connect provider, an LDAP directory and a SAML identity provider, all of which let anyone sign in as anyone; **installation packages** for Docker and Kubernetes.
- **Two test environments** on one machine, each seeded with a tenant, a person and an administrator: the stack in Docker with the three development providers, and Hodor installed by its chart on a Kubernetes cluster in Docker.
- **Two demonstrations**, one per milestone, run in continuous integration against the image.
- **The documentation site**, published from `docs/` at <https://ortisun.github.io/hodor/>.
